# A Multiple-Scattering Microwave Radiative Transfer Model for Land Emission With Vertically Heterogeneous Vegetation Coverage

**Kaiqi Chen**, *Graduate Student Member, IEEE*, and **Shurun Tan**, *Member, IEEE*

**Abstract— A multiple scattering model for passive radiative transfer (RT) in vegetation that accounts for the vertical profile of the plant structure is developed, offering advancements over the commonly used single-layer uniform scattering models prevalent in the vegetated land surface microwave remote sensing. The proposed model takes into account the complexities of the canopy morphology with vertical heterogeneity, enabling the representation of overlapping vegetation species applicable to diverse plant types and growth stages. Additionally, it serves as a valuable tool for understanding the influence of the vegetation vertical structure on the microwave brightness temperatures. The model is constructed based on high-order solutions to the RT equations, obtained through a numerical iterative approach with an efficient interpolation scheme for algorithm acceleration. This methodology facilitates the accurate distinction of the contributions to the brightness temperature from each scattering order and scattering mechanism, ensuring a comprehensive consideration of multiple scattering effects within various vegetated scenarios. The model is validated using the SMAPVEX12 L-band forest dataset, encompassing a wide range of soil moisture variations. Comparisons are made between the brightness temperatures simulated by the newly developed multiple-scattering model with a continuous profile or layered profile and those obtained from a uniform single-layer model. Results demonstrate significant improvements in the multilayered or the continuously profiled model, showing improved agreement with the measured brightness temperatures. Furthermore, the proposed model is parameterized by matching the high-order solutions to the RT equation to the widely adopted reduced order albedo-tau formalism. The resulting equivalent parameters are linked to the geometries and the electromagnetic properties of the vegetation layer, while also incorporating the effects of multiple scattering. Comparative analysis of the equivalent parameters derived from the layered model and those derived from the single-layer model reveals that the vertical heterogeneity of the vegetation structure has a notable influence on the effective scattering albedo and it yields a value more consistent with the albedo as chosen in the SMAP/SMOS inversion algorithms. Meanwhile, the impact of the vegetation vertical profile on the effective optical thickness and the effective transmissivity of the vegetation layer is weak. These insights are essential for the retrieval of soil moisture and vegetation characteristics including the plant vertical structures in microwave remote sensing.**

**Index Terms— Multilayered model, nonuniform vegetation vertical profile, numerical iterative method, passive microwave remote sensing, radiative transfer (RT), the albedo-tau model.**

## I. INTRODUCTION

SOIL moisture has a critical role in the terrestrial water cycle and is essential for monitoring droughts and predicting plant growth. The utilization of passive microwave remote sensing technology is prevalent in the soil moisture observation owing to its high sensitivity to soil moisture [1], [2], [3]. Nevertheless, soils are typically covered by different types of vegetation, and the existence of vegetation introduces its own radiation energy while simultaneously attenuating the emission from the underneath soil. This presents difficulties in retrieving soil moisture from microwave brightness temperatures. Therefore, numerous electromagnetic scattering models have been developed to enhance understanding of microwave signals’ interaction with vegetation components and the soil.

The microwave electromagnetic scattering models for vegetated land surfaces can be categorized into full-wave analysis-based approaches [4], [5], [6], [7], [8] and radiative transfer (RT) theory-based approaches [9], [10], [11]. The full-wave method considers the interaction of coherent waves between the vegetation, taking into account the impact of vertical structure and horizontal distribution characteristics of plants. This approach is considered to provide a more accurate depiction of vegetation scattering properties. However, the full-wave method typically necessitates a large amount of computational resources. Although the recently proposed fast multiple scattering theory has significantly accelerated the computational process, handling coupling between vegetation scattering and rough ground scattering in full-wave analysis still remains a challenge [7], [8]. On the other hand, the RT theory is based on the energy conservation and it accounts for incoherent far-field scattering interactions within the scenes. The RT theory assumes that scatterers are uniformly distributed in the space, therefore, initial models typically assumed that various vegetation components were uniformly distributed above the soil, ignoring the vertical structure of vegetation [12], [13], [14].

In order to introduce the vertical structural features of vegetation into the RT model, the RT model has undergone a development from a two-layer model [9], [15], [16] to a multilayered model [17], [18], [19]. For forest scenarios, the MIMICS model [15] and the TVG model [9], [16] have been proposed successively. According to these models, vegetation is separated into two layers: the canopy layer and the trunk layer, with the trunk being truncated below the canopy layer. Validation and application of these models have been conducted on a single forest stand or on stands with comparable structures [9], [15], [16]. However, these two-layer models are inadequate for complicated forest scenarios, particularly those including mixed forests with varying tree species and growth stages.

In [17], the MIMICS model has been expanded into a multilayered MIMICS model. In order to account for the height-dependent variation of scattering elements in a forest canopy, multiple vegetation layers are employed instead of one. Recently, a multilayer water cloud model [19] is also proposed to better represent the morphological aspects of plants and understand the influence of vertical water distribution on radar backscatter. Nevertheless, these multilayered models ignore the multiple scattering effects within the scene, resulting in a tendency for these models to overestimate the attenuation impact of vegetation. In [20], the TVG model is expanded to account for height-dependent structural variation in an alpine meadow through the implementation of a multi-volume approach where the vertical heterogeneity of a pasture is represented by three segments/layers. That work elucidates the capability of the TVG model to consider the heterogeneity of vegetation vertical structure while taking into account multiple scattering effects within the scene. However, the TVG model lacks physical interpretability regarding the scattering mechanism within the scene, thus unable to separate the contribution of each scattering order and scattering mechanisms, i.e., specific scattering paths, to microwave observations. What is more, the aforementioned multilayered models primarily serve the purpose of simulating the microwave backscatter coefficients. At present, there is an absence of research pertaining to multilayered models for passive microwave remote sensing, and the understanding of the impact of vegetation’s vertical structure on the scene brightness temperatures is limited.

The primary objective of this article is to incorporate the vertical structural heterogeneity of vegetation into the passive radiation transfer theory, while maintaining the capability to handle multiple scattering effects within the scene. In our proposed model, the vegetation is represented with an arbitrary vertical structure, and the multispecie scatterers to represent various structural elements of the vegetation, i.e., the trunk, the branches, and the leaves, are all distributed with a specified vertical profile as described by their geometry, orientation, material properties (as linked to the water contents), and number densities. Such material and geometric distributions of the scatterers are then transformed into the vertical distribution of the electrical scattering properties, as represented by the scattering phase matrices, the absorption, and extinction coefficients, which are subsequently substituted into the RT equations to solve for the scene brightness temperatures.

The effect of multiple scattering in vegetated land surfaces is analyzed by solving higher order solutions to RT equations. Existing models obtain the higher order solutions to RT equations through several methods including the numerical iterative approach [10], [11], [21], the discrete coordinate method with eigenvalue analysis [22], [23], and the matrix doubling method [9], [16]. The numerical iterative approach is adopted in this work following our recent study [24]. The numerical iterative approach as developed in [24] is proven effective and efficient in solving RT equations with irregular phase matrices, as encountered in forest scenarios with electrically large tree trunks and branches accompanying a strongly forward scattering pattern. The singular phase matrices pose difficulties in solving RT equations and is tackled by a forward scattering extraction strategy and an intensity interpolation scheme in [24].

The devised model enables the investigation of the impact of vegetation’s vertical structure on the scene brightness temperatures with multiple scattering effects. Additionally, the model facilitates the observation and analysis of the contribution to the scene brightness temperatures from different scatterer components at varying heights. This model also predicts the linkage between the vegetation vertical structures and the resulting brightness temperatures. Measurement data from forest scenes in the SMAPVEX12 campaign [25] are employed to validate the efficacy of the developed model. The findings to be reported indicate that the vertical structure of vegetation exerts a considerable influence on the brightness temperature arising from the vegetation layer, while changes in vegetation structure have a relatively minimal impact on the brightness temperature contributed from the soil.

While taking into account the vertical structure of vegetation and the multiple scattering effects, the developed forward model becomes more intricate. Simple representations, such as the albedo-tau model (also known as the tau-omega model in literature) [26], are generally preferred from the perspective of inversion due to the less number of parameters involved. There are two key parameters in the albedo-tau model: the single scattering albedo $\omega$, which is defined as the ratio of the scattering coefficient to the extinction coefficient, and the optical thickness tau $\tau$, defined as the product of the scattering coefficient and the vegetation depth. Prior studies have presented equivalent parameters $\omega$ and $\tau$ by fitting simulation results of a multiple scattering model [12], [27] or experimental data [28], [29] into the albedo-tau formalism. The difficulty resides in deriving the coupled two parameters simultaneously in this fitting procedure. There are research that derives the equivalent albedo from the multiple scattering model while relying on the single scattering optical thickness $\tau$ [13], [30]. However, recent research indicates that optical thickness obtained based on single scattering overestimates the attenuation by the vegetation [29], [31], [32]. Therefore, it becomes necessary to incorporate multiple scattering effects into the optical thickness to accurately quantify the transmissivity of the vegetation layer. To fill this research gap, our earlier study [24] proposed a parameterization approach that derives both equivalent parameters by matching the higher order solutions to the RT equation with the albedo-tau model with a well-designed procedure. The work in [24] is rooted in a physical definition of the optical thickness $\tau$ that links to the multiple scattering solution to the passive RT equation. Two specific scenarios are devised to derive the two parameters unambiguously. Meanwhile, there is a lack of research analyzing the influence of vegetation vertical structure on equivalent parameters. Consequently, the parameterization approach developed in [24] is adopted again in this work to derive the equivalent albedo and tau parameters of the vegetation layer with an arbitrary vertical structure. The obtained equivalent parameters, i.e., the equivalent albedo and the equivalent tau, encompass the consideration of vertical heterogeneity and the impact of multiple scattering processes. The sensitivity of these equivalent parameters to soil factors and the influence of the vegetation vertical structure on these equivalent parameters are comprehensively examined in this article. The findings are to offer valuable theoretical support for the microwave inversion of plant structure information and the selection of appropriate equivalent parameters for the soil retrieval algorithm.

This article is structured as follows. The proposed multiple scattering model incorporating the vegetation vertical heterogeneity is presented in Section II. The albedo-tau parameterization of the physical scattering model is introduced in Section II-C. Simulation and analysis of vertical structure of vegetation on the scene brightness temperatures and the equivalent albedo and tau parameters are conducted in Section III. The validation of the microwave emission model using SMAPVEX12 data is reported in Section IV. And finally the conclusions are drawn in Section V.

## II. GENERALIZED MULTIPLE SCATTERING MODEL TO INCORPORATE VEGETATION VERTICAL HETEROGENEITY AND ITS PARAMETERIZATION

### A. Brief Review to the RT Theory and the Numerical Iterative Approach

The RT theory is commonly employed for modeling microwave emissions from vegetated land surfaces. This theory provides a framework for describing the behavior of electromagnetic radiation as it propagates through a random medium, based on the law of energy conservation. Within the RT theory, the vegetation is represented by a collection of discrete scatterers located above the ground, as depicted in Fig. 1. For forest scenes, tree trunks, branches, and leaves are represented by cylinders or disks with varying sizes, orientations, and dielectric constant (primarily linked to the water content).

RT theory assumes that various types of scatterers are uniformly distributed within the vegetation layer, which is treated as a statistically homogeneous scattering and absorbing layer. In the independent scattering assumption, the attenuation,

**Fig. 1.** Scenario illustration of applying RT theory to modeling brightness temperatures for a vegetation covered surface.

absorption, and scattering properties of a single vegetation layer are acquired by incoherent summation over the single scattering characteristics of each scatterer. The vegetation layer scattering properties are represented by the extinction coefficient $\kappa_e$, absorption coefficient $\kappa_a$, and scattering phase matrix $P$, respectively.

The passive RT equation in scalar form is expressed as follows for a vertically statistically homogeneous region:

$$ \cos \theta \frac{d I(z, \theta)}{dz} = -\kappa_e(\theta)I(z, \theta) + \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \kappa_a(\theta)T + \int_0^\pi d\theta' p(\theta, \theta') I(z, \theta') \sin \theta' \tag{1} $$

where

$$ p(\theta, \theta') = \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi' P(\theta, \phi; \theta', \phi') \tag{2} $$

is the reduced scattering phase matrix, where $\theta'$ and $\theta$ represent the incident and scattering inclination angles.

In the passive RT equation, $K_B$ represents the Boltzmann’s constant, $\lambda$ is the free space wavelength, and $T$ is the physical temperature of the vegetation layer. $I(z, \theta)$ is the microwave radiation intensity, where $z$ represents the height above the ground and $\theta$ represents the observation angle. In passive remote sensing of scenarios with statistical azimuthal symmetry, microwave radiation intensity is assumed to be uniform in the azimuthal direction and independent of the azimuth angle $\phi$.

For scattering in the vegetation layer, RT theory with a single reflective boundary at the bottom is commonly adopted [33], as shown in Fig. 1. The boundary conditions are taken as follows:

$$
\begin{cases}
I_d(z = d, \theta) = 0 \\
I_u(z = 0, \theta) = \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2}(1 - r_{12})T_{\text{gnd}} + I_d(z = 0, \theta)r_{12}.
\end{cases}
\tag{3}
$$

In (3), $d$ is the depth of the vegetation layer, $T_{\text{gnd}}$ is the physical temperature of the ground, and $r_{12}$ is the reflectivity matrix at the bottom boundary. In this study, the Kirchhoff approximation [34] is applied to calculate the reflectivity of the soil.

The iterative approach is an effective technique to solve RT equations with multiple scattering effects. It derives from the RT equation in its integral form and numerical iterations are performed to higher orders until convergence is reached. In each iteration, a further order of scattering is incorporated through a numerical integration of the product of the phase matrix and the microwave intensity. The iterative approach enables separations of individual contributions to microwave intensity from each scattering order and scattering mechanism, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of multiple scattering effects in vegetated land surfaces [10], [11], [35].

### B. Generalization to Handle Vertical Heterogeneity of the Vegetation Structure

For complex vegetation scenes, it is essential to improve the model’s depiction of vegetation morphology. As shown in Fig. 2, the height-dependent geometry, composition, and structure of a natural canopy is characterized as elemental scatterers at different heights with varying dielectric constants, sizes, orientations, and distribution densities. Consequently, vegetation can be subdivided into an arbitrary number of sublayers based on its vertical geometric structure. The thickness of each sublayer and the characteristics of the scatterers it contains can be substantially different from other sublayers. Similar to single-layer vegetation models, it is assumed that scatterers within each layer are uniformly distributed. The scattering parameters of each layer are obtained by summing the single scattering characteristics of all scatterers present within that specific layer. Hence, the heterogeneity of the vertical structure of vegetation is manifested further as height-dependent scattering parameters. Such height-dependent scattering parameters are then introduced into the RT equation to make the RT model applicable to complex vegetation structure. The corresponding passive RT equation in scalar form is expressed as follows, incorporating the vertical heterogeneity of scattering parameters:

$$ \cos \theta \frac{d I(z, \theta)}{dz} = -\kappa_e(\theta, z)I(z, \theta) + \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \kappa_a(\theta, z)T(z) + \int_0^\pi d\theta' p(\theta, \theta'; z) I(z, \theta') \sin \theta' \tag{4} $$

where

$$ p(\theta, \theta'; z) = \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi' P(\theta, \phi; \theta', \phi'; z). \tag{5} $$

In (5), the scattering phase matrix is defined as follows:

$$ P(\theta, \phi; \theta', \phi'; z) = \sum_{s=1}^{N_s} n_0^{(s)}(z) \left| f^{(s)}(\theta, \phi; \theta', \phi') \right|^2 \tag{6} $$

where $n_0^{(s)}(z)$ is the density profile of scatterers of the $s$th specie per unit volume at the height of $z$ above the ground, which characterizes the vertical distribution of various scatterer components in vegetation and can be adjusted according to the vegetation morphology. $N_s$ represents the number of scatterer species. $f^{(s)}(\theta, \phi; \theta', \phi')$ is the scattering amplitude of the $s$th scatterer specie, where $\theta'$ and $\theta$ represent the incident and scattering inclination angles, respectively, and $\phi'$ and $\phi$ represent the incident and scattering azimuth angles, respectively. $f^{(s)}(\theta, \phi; \theta', \phi')$ is typically calculated using physical electromagnetic scattering models such as the infinite cylinder approximation (ICA) to model the scattering of tree trunks and branches, and the thin disk approximation (TDA) for simulating the scattering of broad leaves [36].

Here, the scattering coefficient $\kappa_s(\theta, z)$ is obtained by integrating the reduced scattering phase matrix over all scattering angles. The absorption coefficient $\kappa_a(\theta, z)$ is determined by

$$ \kappa_a(\theta, z) = \sum_{s=1}^{N_s} n_0^{(s)}(z) \sigma_a^{(s)}(\theta) \tag{7} $$

where $\sigma_a^{(s)}(\theta)$ is the absorption cross section of a scatterer of the $s$th specie. The absorption cross section is obtained by integrating the internal field inside the scatterer [35]. The extinction coefficient $\kappa_e(\theta, z)$ characterizes the extinction of coherent electromagnetic waves during forward propagation caused by absorption and scattering of scatterers and is estimated by adding the scattering coefficient $\kappa_s(\theta, z)$ and the absorption coefficient $\kappa_a(\theta, z)$.

The integral form of the RT equation with boundary conditions of (3) is written as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_u(z, \theta) &= \int_0^z dz' \exp(\tau(\theta, z') - \tau(\theta, z)) \\
&\quad \times \sec \theta \left[ \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \kappa_a(\theta, z')T(z') + S(z', \theta) \right] \\
&\quad + \sec \theta \exp(-\tau(\theta, z))r_{12} \\
&\quad \times \int_0^d dz' \exp(-\tau(\pi - \theta, z')) \\
&\quad \times \left[ \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \kappa_a(\pi - \theta, z')T(z') + W(z', \theta) \right] \\
&\quad + \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \exp(-\tau(\theta, z))(1 - r_{12})T_{\text{gnd}}
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_d(z, \theta) &= \int_z^d dz' \exp(-\tau(\pi - \theta, z') + \tau(\pi - \theta, z)) \\
&\quad \times \sec \theta \left[ \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \kappa_a(\pi - \theta, z')T(z') + W(z', \theta) \right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$

with

$$
\begin{aligned}
S(z, \theta) = \int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta' \sin \theta' &\left[ p(\theta, \theta'; z)I_u(z, \theta') + p(\theta, \pi - \theta'; z) \right. \\
&\left. \times I_d(z, \theta') \right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(z, \theta) = \int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta' \sin \theta' &\left[ p(\pi - \theta, \theta'; z)I_u(z, \theta') \right. \\
&\left. + p(\pi - \theta, \pi - \theta'; z)I_d(z, \theta') \right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
$$

and

$$ \tau(\theta, z) = \sec(\theta) \int_0^z \kappa_e(\theta, z'') dz'' \tag{12} $$

represents the optical depth from the ground to a specific height $z$. The upward propagating microwave intensity $I_u(z, \theta)$ contains three terms that correspond to upward radiation/scattering from height $z'$ ($z' < z$), downward radiation/scattering from height $z'$ reflected by the ground, and upward emission from the soil. The downward propagating microwave intensity, $I_d(z, \theta)$, represents downward radiation/scattering from height $z'$ ($z' > z$). $S(z, \theta)$ and $W(z, \theta)$ denote the upward and downward equivalent scattering source terms contributed by intensities propagating in other directions to $I_u(z, \theta)$ and $I_d(z, \theta)$, respectively, as coupled by the reduced phase matrix $p(\theta, \theta'; z)$.

The zeroth-order intensities, denoted as $I_u^{(0)}(z, \theta)$ and $I_d^{(0)}(z, \theta)$, are obtained by considering the radiation from the soil and vegetation as the sole contributors while setting the scattering source terms $S(z', \theta)$ and $W(z', \theta)$ in (8) and (9) to be zero. The expressions are shown below

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_u^{(0)}(z, \theta) &= \int_0^z dz' \exp(\tau(\theta, z') - \tau(\theta, z)) \\
&\quad \times \sec \theta \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \kappa_a(\theta, z')T(z') \\
&\quad + \sec \theta \exp(-\tau(\theta, z))r_{12} \\
&\quad \times \int_0^d dz' \exp(-\tau(\pi - \theta, z')) \\
&\quad \times \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \kappa_a(\pi - \theta, z')T(z') \\
&\quad + \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \exp(-\tau(\theta, z))(1 - r_{12})T_{\text{gnd}}
\end{aligned}
\tag{13}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_d^{(0)}(z, \theta) &= \int_z^d dz' \exp(-\tau(\pi - \theta, z') + \tau(\pi - \theta, z)) \\
&\quad \times \sec \theta \frac{K_B}{\lambda^2} \kappa_a(\pi - \theta, z')T(z').
\end{aligned}
\tag{14}
$$

Once the zeroth-order solution has been obtained, the iterative approach is used to compute higher order brightness temperatures. In each iteration, the $n$th-order ($n \ge 1$) of scattering source term $S^{(n)}(z', \theta)$ and $W^{(n)}(z', \theta)$ is obtained by integrating the product of scattering phase matrix and the $(n - 1)$th-order intensities $I_u^{(n-1)}(z, \theta)$ and $I_d^{(n-1)}(z, \theta)$

$$
\begin{aligned}
S^{(n)}(z, \theta) &= \int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta' \sin \theta' \\
&\quad \times \left[ p(\theta, \theta'; z)I_u^{(n-1)}(z, \theta') \right. \\
&\quad \left. + p(\theta, \pi - \theta'; z)I_d^{(n-1)}(z, \theta') \right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
W^{(n)}(z, \theta) &= \int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta' \sin \theta' \\
&\quad \times \left[ p(\pi - \theta, \theta'; z)I_u^{(n-1)}(z, \theta') \right. \\
&\quad \left. + p(\pi - \theta, \pi - \theta'; z)I_d^{(n-1)}(z, \theta') \right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{16}
$$

Then, the $n$th order of upward and downward intensities $I_u^{(n)}(z, \theta)$ and $I_d^{(n)}(z, \theta)$ is updated by the $n$th-order scattering source term $S^{(n)}(z', \theta)$ and $W^{(n)}(z', \theta)$

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_u^{(n)}(z, \theta) &= \int_0^z dz' \exp(\tau(\theta, z') - \tau(\theta, z)) \sec \theta S^{(n)}(z', \theta) \\
&\quad + \exp(-\tau(\theta, z))r_{12} \\
&\quad \times \int_0^d dz' \exp(-\tau(\pi - \theta, z')) \sec \theta W^{(n)}(z', \theta)
\end{aligned}
\tag{17}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_d^{(n)}(z, \theta) &= \int_z^d dz' \exp(-\tau(\pi - \theta, z') + \tau(\pi - \theta, z)) \\
&\quad \times \sec \theta W^{(n)}(z', \theta).
\end{aligned}
\tag{18}
$$

The procedure is repeated iteratively to yield higher order multiple scattering contributions to the brightness temperature until the convergence is reached. The resulting multiple scattering contributions from zeroth order to $N$th order are then summed up to obtain the up to $N$th-order brightness temperature

$$ T_{B, \text{total}}^{(N)}(\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^N T_B^{(n)}(\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^N \frac{I_u^{(n)}(z = d, \theta)}{\frac{K_B}{\lambda^2}}. \tag{19} $$

### C. Albedo-Tau Parameterization of the Generalized Multiple Scattering Model With Vertical Heterogeneity

The $\omega - \tau$ model is frequently applied in inversion algorithms for vegetated land surface due to its simplicity. In our previous work [24], a strategy for parameterizing the RT model with the $\omega - \tau$ formulation is proposed by matching the model brightness temperature predictions in controlled conditions. This yields equivalent parameters of the vegetation layer that account for multiple scattering effects. The scheme is taken in this article to further parameterize the generalized RT model and derive equivalent parameters that account for both the multiple scattering effects and the vertical heterogeneity of vegetation.

The basic idea to parameterize the physical scattering model is to equalize the multiple scattering solution $T_{B, \text{total}}^{(N)}$ to the $\omega - \tau$ formalism

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{B, \text{total}}^{(N)}(\theta) &= T_{\text{gnd}}(1 - r_{12}) \exp(-\tau_{\text{eff}} \sec \theta) \\
&\quad + (1 - \omega_{\text{eff}})T(1 - \exp(-\tau_{\text{eff}} \sec \theta)) \\
&\quad + (1 - \omega_{\text{eff}})T r_{12}(1 - \exp(-\tau_{\text{eff}} \sec \theta)) \\
&\quad \times \exp(-\tau_{\text{eff}} \sec \theta).
\end{aligned}
\tag{20}
$$

To decouple the equivalent parameters $\omega_{\text{eff}}$ and $\tau_{\text{eff}}$, we first set the physical temperature of the vegetation layer $T$ to zero and the ground reflectivity $r_{12}$ to zero, for which the $\omega - \tau$ formalism in (20) is reduced to the first term representing the upward emission from the ground. This allows us to estimate the equivalent transmissivity $t$ as well as the equivalent optical thickness $\tau_{\text{eff}}$ through

$$ t(\theta) = \frac{T_{B, \text{total}}^{(N)}(\theta)}{T_{\text{gnd}}} = \exp(-\tau_{\text{eff}} \sec \theta). \tag{21} $$

With the effective optical thickness $\tau_{\text{eff}}$ being determined, the effective albedo $\omega_{\text{eff}}$ is then obtained through (20). Note the effective albedo $\omega_{\text{eff}}$ derived in this manner depends on the choice of the ground reflectivity $r_{12}$ and the ground temperature $T_{\text{gnd}}$, and the sensitivity of the derived parameter $\omega_{\text{eff}}$ on the chosen ground conditions is to be tested. The effectiveness of this strategy has been validated for a layer of tree trunks above the ground in [24]. The applicability of it in a more realistic forest scenario comprised of multispecie scattering elements with vertical distribution profiles is to be tested in this article. This parameterization provides theoretical foundation for linking the scattering albedo and optical thickness of the vegetation layer to its geometric and material properties. Our study also indicates the potential to invert vegetation vertical structures from the equivalent parameters.

## III. NUMERICAL RESULTS

### A. Simulation Setup and Scattering Characteristics of Tree Components

In order to illustrate the impact of vertical heterogeneity of vegetation on brightness temperatures, realistic simulations are performed in this study. Simulations are conducted at the L-band SMAP passive channel frequency of 1.41 GHz. A land cover of aspen trees is chosen as the scene for simulation analysis using the measurement data from the SMAPVEX12 campaign [25]. In the simulation, trees are considered to be composed of discrete scatterers of trunks, primary branches (PBs), secondary branches (SBs), and circular leaves. Table I displays the information about vegetation components that is applied in the simulation as estimated from in situ measurement. The size and volumetric moisture of trunks and branches are obtained by taking the average of the measurements. The size and density of leaves are reasonably estimated as they are not explicitly reported in the SMAPVEX12 dataset. The SMAPVEX12 campaign provides a canopy thickness for trees of 6 m, specifically referring to the vertical distance from the first branching point to the top of the tree. The estimated tree height is 8 m, which is comparable to the height of the trunk. The number density of trees is determined based on the vegetation water content (VWC) of the aspen sample plot.

In this study, the ICA method [35] is employed to calculate the scattering characteristics of trunks and branches, while the TDA method [35] is applied to calculate the scattering properties of aspen leaves. The dielectric constants of vegetation components are calculated from the dual-dispersion model [37].

### C. Effective Parameters Derived From the Multilayered Model

As described in Section III, the effective optical depth $\tau_{\text{eff}}$ is derived from the physical equivalent transmissivity of the vegetation layer and its value is independent of soil parameters, while the effective albedo $\omega_{\text{eff}}$ can potentially be affected by the choice of the soil parameters. Therefore, the sensitivity analysis of the derived effective albedo $\omega_{\text{eff}}$ to soil parameters is first carried out.

Fig. 9 depicts the equivalent albedo derived from the uniform model and the layered model as a function of the soil moisture and the soil temperature at the observation angle of $40^\circ$. The equivalent albedo is shown to be insensitive to the soil temperature, and weakly affected by the soil moisture parameters, especially for the layered structure cases. Furthermore, it is observed that the equivalent albedo decreases as soil moisture increases, which contrasts with the results reported in [13]. This discrepancy arises because the equivalent albedo in [13] is derived from the single scattering optical thickness rather than the equivalent optical thickness that incorporates the effects of multiple scattering. This comparison highlights the impact of optical thickness on the effective scattering albedo and reaffirms the critical importance of incorporating multiple scattering effects in deriving both parameters.

### B. Continuous Vegetation Vertical Profile Model

In Section III, we have adopted two types of piecewise constant four-layer tree structures to represent the vegetation vertical profile for analysis purposes. In this section, the vegetation canopy is assumed with an arbitrary vertical distribution profile. The distribution density of tree branches and leaves can be obtained by the measured (e.g., from Lidar point clouds) or estimated (e.g., from a vegetation heterogenic growth model) vertical profile of the vegetation, as a function of the height. Due to the absence of field measurement data relative to the vertical profile structure of the forest site F5, we have made reasonable estimations regarding the distribution of vegetation components. Fig. 13 illustrates the normalized distribution density of tree branches and leaves with respect to the height, with the integration of the normalized distribution density resulting in the value of 1. In our estimations, the distribution density of tree branches in the canopy follows a linear decline with increasing height, while the distribution density of leaves exhibits a parabolic relationship with the height. ### C. Results and Discussions

Fig. 15 shows the comparison between the model simulations and the measured brightness temperatures. Green star dots show the simulation results from the uniform model, red circle dots present the simulation results from the layered model with Case 1 structure, while black square dots refer to the simulation results with the continuous profile structure as specified in Fig. 13. The SMAPVEX12 measurements are shown by their dynamic range from minimum to maximum and their mean values in blue lines and blue cross-shaped markers, respectively. The results indicate that the uniform model substantially underestimates the brightness temperature, whereas the layered model estimates comparable brightness temperatures with the measured values. The root-mean-square error (RMSE) between the average measurement values and simulations from the uniform model for both vertical and horizontal polarizations are recorded as 11.20 K and 9.02 K, respectively. However, the RMSE between the averaged measurements and simulations from the continuous profile model is reduced to 3.45 K and 3.42 K for vertical and horizontal polarizations, respectively. This indicates that the vertical structure of the vegetation has a considerable impact on the accuracy of brightness temperature predictions. Additionally, the RMSE between the average measurement values and simulations from the Case 1 staircase structure is calculated as 2.85 K for vertical polarization and 3.64 K for horizontal polarization. This indicates that incorporating vertical continuous profiles into the model is not obligatory for accurate brightness temperature predictions. A simple layered structure, such as a four-layer structure, is sufficient to characterize the impact of vegetation vertical structures on the corresponding brightness temperatures. However, it remains an issue to be examined on how to simplify a continuous vertical profile of the vegetation into staircase layers.

## V. CONCLUSION

In this article, a multiple scattering model based on RT theory incorporating vegetation vertical heterogeneity has been introduced to accurately predict the brightness temperature of vegetated land scenarios especially for forested areas. By considering the effects of multiple scattering and vertical heterogeneity within the vegetation canopy, the proposed model provides a valuable tool for understanding the influence of vertical vegetation structure on the brightness temperature. Incorporating vertical heterogeneity into the model is achieved through the adjustment of the distribution range of vegetation scattering components in the vertical direction, resulting in height-dependent characteristics of vegetation scattering. Within a forest scene, our findings reveal that the vertical structure of vegetation has a substantial impact on the observed brightness temperatures. The brightness temperature contributions from the vegetation layer are primarily influenced by the vertical vegetation structure, while the contributions from the underlying soil are less affected.

The model provides novel and distinctive insights into the individual contribution from a tree component in the scene to microwave intensities at various heights. Our study demonstrates that variations in the vertical distribution range of vegetation components substantially alter their contributions to the intensity at different heights. Notably, the components distributed in the upper layer of vegetation significantly contribute to the scene brightness temperature, particularly emerging as the dominant contributors as the observation angle increases.

The proposed multiple scattering model incorporating vegetation vertical heterogeneity is also parameterized in the form of an equivalent zero-order albedo-tau model. The derived equivalent parameters encompass the impact of multiple scattering and vegetation vertical heterogeneity. Upon considering the effects of multiple scattering, it is observed that vegetation structure has minimal influence on equivalent optical thickness and vegetation transmittance, but has a notable effect on the equivalent albedo. Accounting for vertical heterogeneity leads to a derived equivalent albedo that closely aligns with the values utilized in inversion algorithms applied in the SMAP/SMOS campaign, emphasizing the importance of incorporating vertical structure when determining the albedo values. This suggests that our model establishes a more robust theoretical basis for the selection of inversion parameters. Furthermore, the derived equivalent albedo exhibits variations for different vegetation structures, potentially opening avenues for utilizing the equivalent albedo in vegetation vertical structure inversion.

Validation of the proposed multiple scattering model with vertical heterogeneity is performed using ground measurement data from the SMAPVEX12 mission in forested areas, with the results illustrating that vertical structure significantly alters the predicted brightness temperatures. Moreover, the predictions made by the layered structure model and the continuous profile model closely match the measured brightness temperatures, highlighting the significance of including vertical structure to accurately simulate the scene brightness temperatures.

To examine the potential of utilizing multiple frequencies to retrieve vegetation and soil parameters, it is advisable to evaluate the proposed model and analyze the impacts of vertical structure on brightness temperatures across various frequencies. Additionally, extending the type of vegetation to crops would be interesting. The methodology and results presented in this article will contribute to enhancing the interpretability of the scattering mechanisms in vegetated land surfaces. While this article assumes a homogeneous scene in developing the models and conducting the analysis, scene heterogeneity at a satellite observation scale would introduce more complexities to forward modeling that deserve further investigations.
